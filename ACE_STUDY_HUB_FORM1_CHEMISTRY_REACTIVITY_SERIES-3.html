1. Introduction to Chemistry

Meaning of Chemistry

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the composition, properties and changes of matter. Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Importance and applications of Chemistry

  • Agriculture: soil testing and the manufacture and appropriate use of fertilisers.
  • Medicine: development, manufacture and quality testing of medicines.
  • Food: food processing, preservation and quality control.
  • Industry: manufacture of cement, glass, soap, paints and plastics.
  • Environment: testing water and monitoring air and water pollution.
  • Energy: understanding fuels, batteries and other energy technologies.

Main branches of Chemistry

  • Organic chemistry: study of carbon compounds, especially hydrocarbons and their derivatives.
  • Inorganic chemistry: study of elements and compounds other than most carbon compounds.
  • Physical chemistry: study of energy changes and physical principles in chemical systems.
  • Analytical chemistry: identification and measurement of substances in samples.
  • Biochemistry: study of chemical substances and processes in living organisms.
Key point: Chemistry helps us understand what substances are made of, their properties and how they change.

2. The Chemistry Laboratory

Laboratory safety rules

  1. Follow the teacher’s instructions and read labels before handling substances.
  2. Wear a laboratory coat and eye protection when instructed.
  3. Never taste laboratory chemicals or directly smell them.
  4. Do not eat, drink, run or play in the laboratory.
  5. Keep containers labelled. Never return unused chemicals to their original stock bottles.
  6. Report spills, breakages and injuries to the teacher immediately.
  7. Keep flammable materials away from naked flames.
  8. Wash hands after practical work.
  9. Do not carry out unauthorised experiments.

Four funnels students must not confuse

Look closely at the shape and the lower outlet. Only the dropping funnel and separating funnel have a tap (stopcock). The filter funnel and thistle funnel do not.

1. Filter funnel — NO TAP

Recognise it: wide cone-shaped bowl and a long, narrow stem. A cone of filter paper sits inside the bowl.

Labels: funnel bowl, filter paper, narrow stem.

Use: holds filter paper during filtration; the solid residue stays on the paper while the filtrate passes through the stem.

2. Thistle funnel — NO TAP

Recognise it: a small shallow cup at the top with a very long, straight, narrow tube. It has no tap or stopcock.

Labels: cup-shaped mouth, long narrow tube.

Use: introduces a liquid into a flask through a cork or rubber stopper, for example in a teacher-demonstrated gas preparation apparatus.

3. Dropping funnel — HAS TAP

Recognise it: a tall, usually cylindrical or pear-shaped reservoir, a stopper at the top, and a tap fitted above a narrow outlet.

Labels: stopper, liquid reservoir, tap/stopcock, outlet tube.

Use: adds a liquid to a reaction vessel at a controlled rate. Turning the tap controls the flow; it is not mainly used to separate two liquid layers.

4. Separating funnel — HAS TAP

Recognise it: a pear-shaped or bulb-shaped vessel with a stopper at the top and a tap at the bottom.

Labels: stopper, upper liquid layer, lower liquid layer, tap/stopcock, outlet.

Use: separates immiscible liquids such as oil and water. The denser lower layer is allowed to leave through the tap first.

Quick identification: Filter funnel = cone + filter paper, no tap. Thistle funnel = cup + long straight tube, no tap. Dropping funnel = liquid reservoir + tap to control addition. Separating funnel = pear-shaped body + tap to drain separate liquid layers.

Desiccator

What is a desiccator?

A desiccator is a sealed laboratory container used to keep substances dry or cool a heated substance without allowing it to absorb moisture from the air.

Parts: heavy lid with knob; airtight ground-glass rim; glass body; perforated plate; sample dish; drying agent (desiccant) below the plate.

Use: stores moisture-sensitive chemicals and allows a heated crucible or sample to cool in a dry atmosphere before weighing.

Important: the drying agent absorbs water vapour. The sample rests above it on the plate; it must not touch the drying agent.

Good laboratory practice

Choose apparatus suitable for the job. Do not heat sealed containers. Never point the mouth of a heated test tube towards anyone. Do not handle hot glassware with bare hands. Follow the teacher’s instructions for any heating or chemical handling.

3. Simple Classification of Substances

Pure substances and mixtures

A pure substance has a fixed composition and characteristic properties. An element contains one type of atom. A compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio. A mixture contains two or more substances physically combined in any proportion.

Examples: copper is an element; pure water is a compound; air and muddy water are mixtures.

Methods of separating mixtures

  • Filtration: separates an insoluble solid from a liquid.
  • Decantation: separates a liquid from settled solid, or carefully pours off one liquid layer.
  • Sieving: separates solid particles of different sizes using a mesh.
  • Evaporation: removes solvent to obtain a dissolved solid.
  • Crystallisation: obtains crystals from a solution as it cools or loses some solvent.
  • Simple distillation: obtains a solvent from a solution by evaporation followed by condensation.
  • Fractional distillation: separates miscible liquids with different boiling points.
  • Separating funnel: separates immiscible liquids, such as oil and water.
  • Chromatography: separates mixture components because they move at different rates through a medium.
  • Magnetic separation: separates magnetic material from non-magnetic material.
  • Centrifugation: uses rapid spinning to help separate suspended particles from a liquid.
  • Sublimation: can separate a sublimable solid from a non-sublimable material in a suitable supervised laboratory procedure.

Diagrams for the separation methods

Each diagram shows the main apparatus and the principle of the method. These are schematic learning diagrams and are not instructions for carrying out practical work.

Decantation

Heavier insoluble solid settles; the clear liquid is carefully poured off without disturbing the sediment.

Sieving

Separates solid particles of different sizes. Smaller particles pass through the mesh; larger ones remain.

Evaporation

Removes a solvent from a solution so that the dissolved solid remains. It may not recover the solvent.

Crystallisation

A concentrated solution is allowed to cool or lose some solvent so crystals of the dissolved solid form.

Simple distillation

Separates a solvent from a solution: the solvent evaporates, then condenses and is collected. The dissolved solid remains in the flask.

Fractional distillation — fully labelled apparatus

Purpose: separates a mixture of miscible liquids that have different, often relatively close, boiling points. For example, ethanol and water can be separated by fractional distillation.

How it works: On heating, vapour from the liquid mixture rises into the fractionating column. The column is packed with beads or has plates that provide a large surface for repeated condensation and evaporation. Vapour richer in the more volatile (lower-boiling) liquid passes to the condenser first. In the condenser, the vapour loses heat, condenses to liquid and runs into the receiving flask. Different fractions can be collected over the course of the separation.

Parts and functions:

  • Distillation flask: holds the liquid mixture being separated.
  • Heat source: supplies energy so the liquid can vaporise; practical heating must follow teacher supervision and laboratory safety rules.
  • Fractionating column: sits between the flask and the still head; encourages repeated evaporation and condensation, improving separation.
  • Packing / glass beads: provide a large surface area for vapour to condense and re-evaporate.
  • Thermometer: measures the temperature of vapour entering the side arm; it helps identify which fraction is distilling.
  • Liebig condenser: has an inner tube carrying vapour and an outer water jacket that cools it.
  • Cooling-water inlet: enters at the lower end of the condenser jacket so the jacket fills completely.
  • Cooling-water outlet: leaves at the upper end of the jacket; the water flows in the opposite direction to the vapour/condensate path.
  • Delivery adapter and receiving flask: guide and collect the condensed liquid (the distillate).
Important exam point: Cooling water enters the condenser at the lower inlet and leaves through the upper outlet. Do not confuse these with the vapour path: vapour travels through the inner tube, while cooling water circulates in the surrounding jacket.

Why the column matters: the higher-boiling component tends to condense lower in the column and flow back towards the flask, while the lower-boiling component is more likely to continue upwards. This repeated process makes the vapour reaching the condenser richer in the lower-boiling liquid.

Separating funnel

Separates immiscible liquids. The denser lower layer is drained first through the tap (stopcock).

Magnetic separation

A magnet attracts magnetic substances such as iron from non-magnetic materials.

Sublimation

A sublimable solid changes directly from solid to gas and deposits again as a solid on a cool surface. Teacher demonstration only.

Centrifugation

Rapid spinning helps separate suspended particles from a liquid by making denser particles collect at the bottom.

Schematic filtration arrangement; not drawn to scale.

Chromatography

In paper chromatography, draw the baseline in pencil near the bottom of the paper and place a small sample spot on it. Put a shallow layer of solvent in the container, making sure the solvent level is below the baseline. The solvent rises through the paper. Mark the solvent front—the furthest point reached by the solvent—before it evaporates. Different components travel different distances and form separated spots. The baseline is the starting line; the solvent front is the final distance reached by the solvent.

Important labels: baseline = pencil line where the sample is first spotted; solvent level = liquid in the container, kept below the baseline; solvent front = furthest point reached by the solvent. The separated spots appear between the baseline and solvent front.

4. Acids, Bases and Indicators

Acids

Acids produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), in water. Examples include hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid and ethanoic acid. Acidic solutions have pH values below 7 at room temperature.

Bases and alkalis

A base neutralises an acid. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water to produce hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq). Alkaline solutions have pH values above 7. A neutral solution has pH 7 at room temperature.

5. Air and Combustion

Composition of air

Clean, dry air is a mixture of gases. Its approximate composition by volume is:

  • Nitrogen: 78%
  • Oxygen: 21%
  • Other gases: about 1%, mainly argon, with carbon dioxide and trace gases.
Composition of clean, dry air by volume. The tiny 1% sector is identified with a leader line so it is not mistaken for a larger share.

Important: These percentages describe clean, dry air. Water vapour is present in varying amounts depending on humidity, while dust and smoke are impurities rather than fixed components of clean, dry air.

Uses of gases in air

  • Oxygen: supports respiration and combustion.
  • Nitrogen: used in manufacture of ammonia and dilutes oxygen in air.
  • Carbon dioxide: used by green plants during photosynthesis.
  • Noble gases: used in lighting and specialised applications.

Phosphorus, oxygen and the composition of air

Phosphorus is a non-metal that reacts with oxygen. The products depend on the supply of oxygen. In a plentiful supply of oxygen, phosphorus(V) oxide is formed. In a limited supply of oxygen, phosphorus(III) oxide may be formed.

OxideFormulaCondition of formationUseful exam fact
Phosphorus(III) oxide (often called phosphorus trioxide)P₄O₆ (empirical formula P₂O₃)Limited supply of oxygenDo not confuse the molecular formula P₄O₆ with the empirical formula P₂O₃.
Phosphorus(V) oxide (often called phosphorus pentoxide)P₄O₁₀ (empirical formula P₂O₅)Plentiful / excess oxygenThe molecular formula is P₄O₁₀; P₂O₅ is its empirical formula.

Balanced equations:

  • Plentiful oxygen: P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀
  • Limited oxygen: P₄ + 3O₂ → P₄O₆

Phosphorus(V) oxide reacts with water to form phosphoric(V) acid: P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄. Phosphorus(III) oxide reacts with water to form phosphorous acid: P₄O₆ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₃.

Storage of phosphorus and other reactive substances

  • White (yellow) phosphorus: stored under water because it can ignite spontaneously in air at ordinary temperatures. Water keeps it away from oxygen. It must only be handled by trained staff in an appropriate laboratory.
  • Red phosphorus: is much less reactive than white phosphorus and is normally kept in a cool, dry, well-labelled container, away from heat and ignition sources. It is not stored under water in the usual school-laboratory description.
  • Sodium and potassium: commonly stored under paraffin oil (or suitable mineral oil) to prevent contact with air and moisture. They are not stored under water because they react dangerously with it.
KCSE distinction: White phosphorus → under water. Sodium / potassium → under paraffin oil. Do not write that phosphorus is stored in paraffin oil.

Determining the approximate percentage of oxygen in air

A traditional school demonstration uses the fact that phosphorus reacts with oxygen in an enclosed sample of air. As oxygen is removed, the gas volume falls and water rises into the space it occupied. The diagram identifies the apparatus and the scientific idea only; phosphorus is hazardous and this demonstration must never be attempted by learners. A qualified teacher should use an approved demonstration method and risk controls.

Conceptual view: oxygen is consumed; the remaining gas occupies less volume and water rises. Apparatus dimensions and water-level change are not to scale.

Explanation, result and limitations

  • Phosphorus combines with oxygen in the enclosed air to form an oxide.
  • The oxygen volume decreases; water rises to occupy part of the space left by the oxygen.
  • The observed decrease is approximately one-fifth of the original air volume, supporting the conclusion that air contains about 21% oxygen by volume.
  • The result is approximate because air also contains other gases, readings may be affected by temperature and pressure changes, and the reaction may not remove all the oxygen.
  • Leaks, an inaccurate volume scale, incomplete reaction and changes in water vapour can also affect the result.

Combustion

Combustion is a chemical reaction in which a substance reacts with oxygen and releases energy, usually as heat and often light.

The fire triangle represents the three conditions needed for ordinary combustion: fuel, oxygen and sufficient heat. Removing any one of these conditions can stop a fire.

The fire triangle.

Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water. Incomplete combustion may produce carbon monoxide and/or soot. Carbon monoxide is poisonous.

Measuring the oxygen used during rusting

Iron reacts slowly with oxygen in the presence of water to form rust. In a school demonstration, the decrease in gas volume in a sealed measuring arrangement can be used to estimate the oxygen removed from air. A two-gas-syringe arrangement is one way of showing the volume change: the syringes are connected through a sealed linking tube, and the rusting iron wool is held in the gas space of the apparatus. The drawing below identifies the parts and the measurement idea; it is not a construction or operating guide.

The two graduated syringes and sealed connector represent a closed gas-volume measuring system. Iron wool is shown in one gas space. Labels identify the plunger, barrel, scale, connecting tube and iron wool.

What the result means

  • Oxygen in the trapped air is gradually used as iron rusts; the gas volume therefore decreases.
  • For dry air, oxygen is about one-fifth (approximately 21%) by volume. A volume decrease approaching one-fifth is the expected idea when oxygen is the gas being removed.
  • Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, often represented as Fe₂O₃·xH₂O; its exact water content varies.

Limitations and sources of error

  • Rusting is slow, so the demonstration may take a long time before the volume change becomes clear.
  • Leaks at the joints allow air to enter or escape, making the volume reading unreliable.
  • Temperature changes cause the gas to expand or contract, even if the amount of gas has not changed.
  • If the iron is not sufficiently moist, rusting may be slow; if conditions vary, results may differ.
  • Other gases in air are not removed in the same way, and the final reading may not equal exactly 21% because of experimental error.
  • The plunger may stick or readings may be affected by parallax; readings should be interpreted carefully by the teacher.

Rusting of iron and its prevention

Rusting is the slow corrosion of iron in which iron reacts with oxygen in the presence of water or moisture to form reddish-brown rust. Both oxygen and water are necessary. Salt solutions generally speed up rusting because they increase electrical conductivity.

Word equation: iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).

Formula: rust is commonly represented as Fe₂O₃·xH₂O. The value of x is variable because rust is hydrated and not a single fixed-composition pure compound.

Exam conclusion: Iron does not rust in completely dry air (water is absent) or in oxygen-free water (oxygen is absent). Rusting requires both oxygen and water.

How to distinguish common protective methods in KCSE questions

  • Painting, oiling and plastic coating: barrier methods; they work only while the coating is continuous.
  • Galvanising: zinc coating; zinc protects by acting as a barrier and by corroding preferentially when iron is exposed.
  • Tin plating: barrier protection while intact; a scratch may cause faster local rusting of exposed iron.
  • Sacrificial protection: a more reactive metal is attached and corrodes instead of iron.
  • Stainless steel: corrosion resistance comes from a protective chromium-rich oxide film formed by the alloy.

2. Explain why a candle enclosed in a gas jar eventually goes out. (3 marks)
The limited oxygen in the enclosed air is used up/reduced below the level needed to support combustion (2); combustion stops and the flame goes out (1).
3. Name the three conditions necessary for combustion and explain how removing one condition extinguishes a fire. (4 marks)
Fuel, oxygen and sufficient heat (3). Removing any one breaks the fire triangle and stops combustion (1).
4. Distinguish between complete and incomplete combustion of a carbon-containing fuel. (4 marks)
Complete combustion occurs with sufficient oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water (2). Incomplete combustion occurs with insufficient oxygen and may produce carbon monoxide and/or soot plus water (2).
5. Give two causes of rusting and two methods of preventing it. (4 marks)
Rusting requires oxygen and water/moisture (2). Prevention: painting, oiling/greasing, galvanising, plastic coating or keeping dry (any two, 1 each).

6. Water, Oxygen and Hydrogen

Water as a substance

Water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen, with formula H₂O. Pure water is colourless, odourless and tasteless. At standard atmospheric pressure, pure water freezes at 0°C and boils at 100°C.

Water treatment

Water intended for drinking is treated to remove suspended particles and harmful microorganisms. Common stages include:

  1. Screening: removes large objects such as leaves and pieces of debris.
  2. Sedimentation: allows heavier suspended particles to settle.
  3. Filtration: passes water through a filter medium to remove smaller suspended particles.
  4. Disinfection: destroys or inactivates harmful microorganisms using an approved treatment method.

The exact treatment process varies with the source and quality of the water.

Hard and soft water

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and/or magnesium ions and does not lather readily with soap. Soft water lathers readily with soap and contains little of these ions.

  • Temporary hardness: commonly caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium hydrogencarbonates; it can be removed by boiling.
  • Permanent hardness: commonly caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium sulfates or chlorides; boiling does not remove it.
Remember: Hard water can form scum with soap and scale in kettles and boilers.

Composition of water

Water is a compound made from hydrogen and oxygen chemically combined in a fixed proportion. Its chemical formula is H₂O. Each molecule contains two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom. The elements cannot be separated by ordinary physical methods such as filtration or evaporation; chemical decomposition is required.

Pure water is neutral, has a pH of about 7 at room temperature, and is a poor conductor of electricity when completely pure. Water from taps, rivers and wells may conduct because it contains dissolved ions.

Water formed when a candle burns

Candle wax is a hydrocarbon (a substance containing carbon and hydrogen). During complete combustion, wax reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water vapour, releasing heat and light.

Word equation: wax + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Evidence for water: water vapour is one of the products of combustion. When the hot combustion products meet a sufficiently cool surface, some water vapour condenses into tiny liquid droplets. This demonstrates that water is produced when a hydrocarbon burns in oxygen.

Shaded schematic showing water droplets condensing on a cool surface above a burning candle. The illustration is not to scale.
Exam point: The droplets are evidence of water vapour in the products of combustion. They are not melted candle wax.

Burning candle in a gas jar: oxygen in air

A candle flame goes out when the oxygen available in the enclosed air is no longer sufficient to support continued burning. In the traditional teacher demonstration, a candle is enclosed in a gas jar standing over water. The water level rises inside the jar as the oxygen is used up and the gases cool. The rise is approximately one-fifth of the original air volume under idealised conditions, showing that air contains about 21% oxygen by volume.

Limitations: the rise is not always exactly one-fifth. Carbon dioxide dissolves in water, the gas cools, and the candle may go out before all oxygen is removed. The experiment gives an approximate result, not an exact measurement.

Conceptual diagram of the candle-and-gas-jar experiment. Water level inside rises as oxygen is consumed and the enclosed gas cools.

Oxygen: laboratory preparation and collection (theory)

Oxygen can be produced in a school laboratory by the catalytic decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. Manganese(IV) oxide is a catalyst: it increases the rate of decomposition and is not used up in the reaction. The products are water and oxygen.

Word equation: hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen

Balanced symbol equation: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)

Why oxygen can be collected over water

  • Oxygen is only slightly soluble in water, so a useful amount can be collected by displacement of water.
  • The gas collects in the inverted gas jar and displaces water from it.
  • The collected gas is not necessarily perfectly dry because it has been in contact with water.

Other collection method: displacement of air

Oxygen is slightly denser than air. In a suitable dry-gas collection arrangement, it can be collected by upward displacement of air (also called downward delivery): oxygen, being slightly denser than air, is introduced into the lower part of an upright gas jar and pushes air upwards and out. This method is useful when collection over water is unsuitable, but the gas may mix with air, so the collection may be less pure.

Hydrogen: occurrence and preparation (theory)

Hydrogen is the lightest element. It is found combined with other elements in substances such as water and many organic compounds. It occurs in very small amounts as a free gas in Earth's atmosphere.

In school chemistry, hydrogen is commonly described as being formed when a suitable metal above hydrogen in the reactivity series reacts with a dilute, non-oxidising acid. The products are a salt and hydrogen gas. The following are alternative reactant pairs that may appear in examination questions; they are equations to learn, not instructions for carrying out a reaction.

General word equation: metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen

Metal and acidSalt formedBalanced equationExam note
Zinc + dilute hydrochloric acidZinc chlorideZn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂Common textbook example.
Zinc + dilute sulfuric acidZinc sulfateZn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂Another standard preparation equation.
Magnesium + dilute hydrochloric acidMagnesium chlorideMg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂Magnesium is more reactive; the reaction is more vigorous than with zinc.
Iron + dilute hydrochloric acidIron(II) chlorideFe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂Iron reacts less readily; iron(II), not iron(III), chloride is the expected salt in this reaction.

Which metals are suitable? Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series can displace hydrogen from dilute hydrochloric or dilute sulfuric acid. Zinc and iron are common textbook examples. Magnesium can also be represented in equations, although its reaction is more vigorous. Aluminium is not a convenient classroom example because its protective oxide coating can hinder reaction.

Important exclusions and cautions for exam answers:

  • Copper, silver and gold: they are below hydrogen in the reactivity series and do not liberate hydrogen from dilute hydrochloric or dilute sulfuric acid.
  • Nitric acid: it is an oxidising acid and generally does not give the usual hydrogen-preparation reaction with metals; do not substitute it in the general equation.
  • Concentrated sulfuric acid: it is oxidising and does not behave like dilute sulfuric acid in the standard hydrogen preparation.
  • Very reactive metals: their reactions with acids or water can be dangerously vigorous and are not suitable examples for learner experiments.

Collection: Hydrogen is only slightly soluble in water, so it may be collected over water in a teacher-supervised demonstration. Since hydrogen is less dense than air, the textbook alternative is downward displacement of air (upward delivery): hydrogen rises into an inverted gas jar and pushes air downwards. This alternative can give a gas mixed with air, so purity is a limitation.

  1. Manufacture of ammonia for fertiliser production.
  2. Hydrogenation of some vegetable oils to make products with different texture and properties.
  3. Use as a fuel in certain fuel cells and specialised applications.
  4. Industrial manufacture and processing of chemicals.

Important comparison: hydrogen and oxygen from water

When water is chemically decomposed by electrolysis, hydrogen is produced at the negative electrode (cathode) and oxygen at the positive electrode (anode). The volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen when measured at the same temperature and pressure.

7. Reactivity Series of Metals

The reactivity series is an arrangement of metals in order of decreasing tendency to lose electrons and form positive ions. A metal near the top is generally more reactive than one below it. The series helps us predict reactions with water, steam, dilute acids and solutions of metal salts, and it helps explain how metals are extracted from their ores.

Important accuracy note: There is no short list containing every metal known. The first list below is the commonly taught school reactivity series; the extended reference includes additional metals. Carbon and hydrogen are included as reference points, but neither is a metal.

1. Common school reactivity series

Read from top to bottom: reactivity generally decreases. Mercury (Hg), silver (Ag) and gold (Au) are shown together at the bottom for a simplified school overview; in a more detailed electrochemical ordering, their exact positions are distinct.

Common order to memorise: Potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, carbon, zinc, iron, tin, lead, hydrogen, copper, mercury, silver, gold.

2. Extended reference series

For broader chemistry, additional metals can be placed in the series. A useful approximate order from more reactive to less reactive is:

Li, K, Ba, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Mn, Zn, Cr, Fe, Co, Ni, Sn, Pb, (H), Cu, Hg, Ag, Pt, Au

This extended list is a reference, not a requirement to memorise every entry for every Form One course. Exact tabulations can differ slightly according to the reactions or standard electrode potentials being compared. The school series above is the key pattern for introductory questions.

3. What does “more reactive” mean?

  • Metals react by losing electrons to form positive ions (cations).
  • A more reactive metal loses electrons more readily than a less reactive metal under comparable conditions.
  • More reactive metals are generally stronger reducing agents: they can transfer electrons to ions of less reactive metals.
  • Reactivity is not the same as how quickly a visible reaction appears. A protective oxide layer, particle size, temperature and concentration can affect the observed rate.

5. Reactions with dilute acids

Metals above hydrogen in the school series generally react with dilute hydrochloric acid or dilute sulfuric acid to form a salt and hydrogen gas. Metals below hydrogen generally do not release hydrogen from these dilute, non-oxidising acids.

Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen

Examples:

  • Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
  • Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
  • Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂
  • Cu + dilute HCl → no reaction (copper is below hydrogen).

Important exceptions and cautions: This prediction is for dilute, non-oxidising acids. Nitric acid is an oxidising acid and usually does not produce hydrogen in the simple way shown. Lead may become coated with an insoluble salt in some acids, slowing or stopping further reaction. Aluminium may appear unreactive at first because of its protective oxide layer.

6. Displacement reactions

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. A less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one. This is a key application of the series.

ExampleObservation / conclusion
Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + CuZinc displaces copper because zinc is above copper. The blue solution fades as copper(II) ions are removed; copper is deposited.
Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + CuIron displaces copper. The blue solution changes towards pale green and copper is deposited.
Cu + ZnSO₄ → no reactionCopper cannot displace zinc because copper is less reactive than zinc.

Ionic idea: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). Zinc atoms lose electrons; copper(II) ions gain them.

7. Reactivity and extraction of metals

  • Very reactive metals such as potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium and aluminium are difficult to extract by heating their ores with carbon. They are commonly extracted by electrolysis of suitable molten compounds.
  • Metals below carbon in the school series, such as zinc, iron, tin, lead and copper, can often be extracted from suitable ores by reduction with carbon or carbon monoxide. The exact industrial process depends on the ore.
  • Very unreactive metals such as gold may occur naturally in the uncombined state.
  • Carbon is placed in the series as a reference because it can remove oxygen from oxides of some metals below it. Hydrogen is a reference because metals above it can generally release hydrogen from dilute non-oxidising acids.

8. Link between reactivity and corrosion

More reactive metals tend to oxidise more readily, but corrosion depends on the environment and protective surface layers. Aluminium is reactive yet often resists further corrosion because a thin, adherent aluminium oxide layer protects the surface. Iron forms rust, which is porous and does not protect the underlying metal effectively.

9. How to answer reactivity-series questions

  1. Locate both metals (or the metal and hydrogen) in the series.
  2. Compare their positions: the one higher up is generally more reactive.
  3. For displacement, a metal higher in the series displaces a metal lower in the series from its salt solution.
  4. For dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, a metal above hydrogen generally releases hydrogen gas.
  5. For extraction, check the metal's position relative to carbon to predict whether carbon reduction is feasible or electrolysis is needed.
Safety: Potassium and sodium react dangerously with water. Highly reactive metals and acid reactions must only be demonstrated by a qualified teacher using approved laboratory procedures. Never try these reactions at home.

Reactivity series — structured examination practice

1. Define the reactivity series of metals. (2 marks)
An arrangement of metals (1) in order of decreasing reactivity / tendency to lose electrons and form positive ions (1).
2. Explain why zinc displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate solution but copper does not displace zinc from zinc sulfate solution. (4 marks)
Zinc is above copper in the reactivity series (1), so zinc loses electrons more readily (1) and displaces Cu²⁺ ions (1). Copper is less reactive than zinc and therefore cannot displace Zn²⁺ ions (1).
3. Write a balanced equation for the reaction between magnesium and dilute hydrochloric acid. State the gas produced. (3 marks)
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ (2). Hydrogen gas (1).
4. Explain why copper does not produce hydrogen when placed in dilute hydrochloric acid. (2 marks)
Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series (1), so it cannot displace hydrogen from dilute hydrochloric acid (1).
5. Explain why sodium is not extracted from its compounds by heating the ore with carbon. (3 marks)
Sodium is more reactive than carbon (1); its compounds are too stable to be reduced by carbon under the usual extraction conditions (1); electrolysis of a suitable molten compound is used instead (1).
6. Distinguish between the reaction of calcium and magnesium with cold water. (4 marks)
Calcium reacts with cold water at a noticeable/moderate rate to form calcium hydroxide and hydrogen (2). Magnesium reacts extremely slowly with cold water because of its lower reactivity and surface effects; it reacts more readily with hot water/steam (2).
7. State two reasons why the observed rate of reaction may not always appear to follow the reactivity series exactly. (2 marks)
Any two: protective oxide coating; different surface areas; temperature; acid concentration; physical state/contact; formation of an insoluble coating (1 each).
8. Iron is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. Name the products and explain the colour change. (4 marks)
Iron(II) sulfate and copper are formed (2). Iron displaces copper because it is more reactive (1); blue Cu²⁺ solution changes towards pale green due to Fe²⁺ ions (1).
